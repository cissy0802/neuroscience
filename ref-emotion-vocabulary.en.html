Emotion Vocabulary& granularity training

Most people describe themselves all day with four or five words on rotation: annoyed, tired, fine, kind of bad. Not because there is less going on — but because feelings you have no words for stay stuck together. That part is trainable.

Be clear about what you're training

How finely you can tell your own feelings apart is called emotional granularity. It gets mistaken for a vocabulary contest — whoever knows the most emotion words wins. That isn't it.

There is only one real test: two words are genuinely distinct only when they point to a different next move. "Disappointed" and "angry" are both unpleasant, but disappointment says what I expected didn't happen, and what needs adjusting is the expectation; anger says somebody crossed a line, and what needs attention is the line. Fail to separate them and you'll bring anger's tools to a disappointment, then wonder why nothing works.

So the list below is not for memorising. The sentence after each word is the actual content: when it's this one rather than the one next to it.

The list

Sorted by the two body readings, plus two groups about me and about other people. A few entries are borrowed words English never coined for itself (schadenfreude, saudade) — worth keeping, because a word a language has and its neighbours don't is usually exactly the distinction its speakers practised.

  • aggrievedIt was unfair, and there's nowhere to say so
  • resentfulAn old debt, still warm every time you think of it
  • contemptuousYou've quietly downgraded the other person — the most corrosive one in a relationship
  • exasperatedAnger wrapped around hope: you only feel it about people you care about

  • panickedYou've decided you can't cope; judgement starts dropping out
  • apprehensiveWaiting for news that hasn't come

The pairs people mix up

Start with these — they're the ones whose next moves diverge most. Guilt versus shame may be the single most valuable distinction on the whole page:

anxiety vs fear
Fear has a target; anxiety doesn't. Ask yourself what you're afraid of: if you can name a specific thing, it's fear; if you can't, it's anxiety — and what needs handling is the uncertainty itself, not the thing.
anger vs frustration
Anger points at "someone crossed a line", frustration at "the road is blocked". Fury at a form that won't submit is frustration; spend it as anger on a person and you damage a bystander.
sadness vs disappointment
Sadness is losing what you had; disappointment is not getting what you expected. Disappointment asks you to revise an expectation, sadness asks you to mourn — swap the methods and one turns into suppression, the other into endless arguing.
guilt vs shame
Guilt says "the thing I did was bad", shame says "I am bad". The first drives repair, the second drives concealment. Most cases of "I just cannot bring myself to apologise" are stuck on shame, not guilt.
envy vs jealousy
Envy is "I want what you have" — a two-person matter. Jealousy is "I'm afraid of losing what I have" — always a third party. Admitting jealousy is much harder, and only naming it correctly tells you what you're trying to protect.
tired vs burned out
Tired resolves with sleep. Burnout doesn't, and it carries something extra: detachment — you stop wanting to care about things you did care about. Burnout isn't a rest deficit, it's a long-unbalanced ledger.
bored vs empty
Bored is "I want to do something but nothing is right" — the drive is intact. Empty is that the wanting itself is gone. Changing activity fixes the first and does nothing for the second.
nervous vs excited
The body hands you nearly the identical reading; the difference is whether you expect a threat or an opportunity. Which is why telling yourself "I'm excited" genuinely works — you aren't lying, you're picking a cheaper explanation for the same state.

How to train it: a four-week version

STEP 0 · BEFORE EVERY NAMING

Thirty seconds auditing the ledger

Hungry? Thirsty? Short on sleep? Sitting for hours? Too much caffeine? Coming down with something? If any answer is yes, write it down first.

A lot of what gets filed as emotion is a body-budget readout wearing the wrong label. This step doesn't deny the feeling — it stops you fighting a budget problem as if it were a relationship problem. → Body budget

STEP 1 · TWICE A DAY, ONE MINUTE EACH

Three questions

Ask the body first. Where is there sensation right now? Tight chest, heavy stomach, shoulders up, dry eyes, clenched jaw — describe only, don't explain.

Then the two axes. Pleasant or unpleasant? Activated or quiet?

Then the word. What's the first one that shows up?

STEP 2 · THE MOST IMPORTANT ONE

The second-word rule

The first word is almost always a coarse label: "annoyed", "bad", "off". Make yourself find a more accurate one, then run the test: do these two words imply the same next move?

Same → you haven't actually separated them; go again. Different → you just learned something.

STEP 3 · ONCE A DAY

Build one complete sentence

"I feel ____, because ____, and I want to ____."

Emotion, trigger, action tendency. If the third blank won't fill, the word in the first one usually isn't precise enough yet — go back and find the second word.

STEP 4 · TWO PER WEEK

Claim new words, and spend them

Pick two words from the list above that you don't normally use, and use them out loud within the week — said to someone, or written in a message.

A word you only recognise isn't learned. Concepts grow through use; a word you've spent once is the kind that shows up on its own next time.

TIMING

Don't practise at peak intensity

High arousal is exactly when prefrontal cortex is least available — practise there and you'll only get frustrated. Train at low to moderate intensity: mildly irritated, slightly flat, just after something eased.

At the peak, do one thing only: put attention back on breath and body, and name it once it comes down.

BACKGROUND TASK

Grow the concept library: novels beat explainers

Emotion concepts grow out of language. A definition tells you what a word means; novels and long interviews show you the situations it gets used in — which is the kind of example the brain actually needs.

Week 1
Step 0 and Step 1 only. The goal is the habit, not accuracy. Rough entries are fine.
Week 2
Add the second-word rule. At least once a day, find the more accurate word.
Week 3
Add the full sentence and two new words a week. Start applying the test: do these two imply the same next move?
Week 4
Reread week one. Most people find that the days they'd flattened into "annoyed" now split into three or four different things — with completely different responses.

What it isn't

It isn't treatment. Low mood, broken sleep, no interest in anything, feeling worthless — running longer than two weeks, naming practice is not the main help you need. Get professional support. This is an adjunct, not a substitute.

Some people start much further back. Real difficulty identifying and describing your own emotions is a trait called alexithymia. It's not an illness but a continuous dimension, and it isn't rare. Starting from words is demoralising here; starting from body sensation is far easier — get reliable at "my chest is tight" before worrying what to call it.

Don't let it become rumination. Naming means attaching a label and putting it down, not circling a feeling for three hours in search of the perfect word. If a minute doesn't produce one, leave it — that isn't a failure.

The evidence says "helps", not "stops it hurting". Higher granularity predicts better regulation, less drinking to cope, steadier stress physiology — but mostly from correlational work and modestly sized intervention trials. The direction is clear; don't expect a large effect.
