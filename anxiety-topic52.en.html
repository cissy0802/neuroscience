The neuroscience of anxiety

Fear has a target, anxiety doesn't: a prediction machine always bracing for "maybe"

2026-10-30 · BigCat

What's hardest to bear often isn't the bad thing itself, but not knowing whether it will come.

Walking down an unlit alley at night, you hear a sudden noise behind you. Your heart jumps; you turn, it's a cat, and within seconds you've settled. That's fear. Tomorrow you get your medical test results; you sleep badly all night, can't say exactly what you're afraid of, and your chest stays tight. That's anxiety.

The two are often lumped together, but the brain handles them somewhat differently. Anxiety isn't simply "being timid" or "overthinking" either: it's a genuinely useful system dedicated to preparing for things that haven't happened yet but might. The trouble comes when a few of its parts are mistuned: uncertainty is felt as too dangerous, avoidance and worry buy short-lived relief, and the "you're safe now" signal doesn't get through.

// 01

Fear has a target, anxiety doesn't

Over decades of experiments in rats, the American neuroscientist Michael Davis and colleagues drew a classic distinction. When a light comes on and a shock follows right away, that clear, imminent threat is handled mainly by the central nucleus of the amygdala: the response comes fast and fades as soon as the threat is gone. But when nobody knows if or when the shock will come, that vague, unpredictable threat relies more on a small structure next to the amygdala called the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST): the response builds slowly but lasts. Amygdala

The distinction has human counterparts. Anti-anxiety drugs work better on the "sustained" response in rats, and the abnormalities of anxiety patients show up more readily in "you don't know when it's coming" experiments than in "the light comes on, then the shock" ones. Later research suggests the two structures' division of labour is less absolute than first proposed, and they often work together; but "imminent and clear" and "vague and sustained" are two different threat states, and that part holds up.

One more thing to be clear about: anxiety itself isn't an illness. A bit of nerves before an exam, double-checking the door before a long trip: that's the system working normally, preparing you for possible trouble. It only becomes a problem when it's too strong, lasts too long, and clearly gets in the way of life.

// 02

The most stressful odds aren't 100%; they're 50%

In 2016 Archy de Berker and colleagues at University College London had 45 volunteers play a game of turning over rocks: a snake might be underneath, and finding one meant a mild electric shock, while the odds of a snake quietly shifted. People were most stressed not when a snake was certain, but when it was fifty-fifty: subjective stress, pupil size and sweaty palms all tracked how uncertain things were, peaking at 50% and lowest at 0% and 100%. Knowing for sure you'd be shocked was actually less distressing.

This suggests anxiety is at heart a prediction problem. Topic 1 described the brain as a machine that never stops predicting. Reviewing anxiety research in 2013, Jack Grupe and Jack Nitschke concluded that anxious people tend to overestimate the probability and the cost of bad outcomes, and find "not knowing" especially hard to bear. Psychology has a questionnaire for exactly this, called "intolerance of uncertainty", and it predicts how severe anxiety is fairly reliably.

The body gets pulled into these predictions too. A racing heart and a knotted stomach reach the insula first, then the brain "reads" them as some emotion. Insula & interoception One classic explanation of panic attacks is that a normal rise in heart rate gets read as "I'm having a heart attack": the scarier the reading, the faster the heart, and the faster the heart, the scarier the reading, in a spiral.

AI parallel

Reinforcement learning (teaching AI to act through trial, error and reward) has a branch called distributional reinforcement learning: instead of estimating just "how much will I score on average", the model learns the whole distribution of possible scores, good and bad alike. On top of that you can build risk-sensitive strategies, for instance making decisions by looking only at the worst slice of that distribution, its bad tail. Such an AI is very safe, but it can also become timid: to avoid a small chance of a bad outcome, it gives up many likely good ones, which looks a lot like anxiety's over-defence. A 2020 study even found that mouse dopamine neurons come in "optimists" and "pessimists", as if jointly encoding a whole distribution of rewards. The difference: an AI's degree of pessimism is a tunable parameter, while a person's is wound together from experience, constitution and current state.

// 03

Avoidance and worry: two habits that keep anxiety alive

The anxious mind's most natural move is to steer clear: skip the party, don't take the call, take the long way round. Each time, anxiety drops at once, and that breath of relief is itself a reward. Psychology calls this negative reinforcement: taking away something unpleasant also strengthens a behaviour. So next time you want to avoid even more.

The hidden cost sits in the lower left: you never get to check that it wasn't so dangerous after all. The brain's prediction that "this is terrifying" is never tested against reality, so it's never updated.

Worry is another, harder-to-spot form of avoidance. The psychologist Thomas Borkovec found that worry mostly means "talking through" the worst case in words inside your head, rather than vividly "seeing" it; he argued that this abstract, verbal style actually damps down the strong emotion that images and bodily sensations would bring, giving a sense of "dealing with it" while really going around it. Later, Michelle Newman and colleagues proposed a revision: worry doesn't lower tension; it keeps you at a steady, moderate level of tension so that bad news can't send your emotions suddenly "off a cliff". What the two accounts share: worry gives a sense of control without the problem or the emotion actually being processed.

// 04

Why "you're safe now" doesn't get through

Once a fear has been learned, can it slowly be let go? In the lab this is called extinction: the light comes on again and again, but the shock never follows. Fear gradually weakens. The key finding is that extinction doesn't erase the original fear memory; it adds a new one: "here, this signal is safe". The old memory is still there, which is why fear can "relapse" in a new setting or after some time has passed.

What remembers the "safe"? In 2002 Mohammed Milad and Gregory Quirk found that neurons in one part of the rat medial prefrontal cortex fire when rats recall extinction learning, and the stronger they fire, the less afraid the rats are. The corresponding human region is the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, which acts like a brake, telling the amygdala "you can relax now". Prefrontal cortex

This is often where anxiety goes wrong. A 2015 meta-analysis of 44 studies found that, compared with other people, anxiety patients showed more fear to signals that plainly meant safety, and their extinction was slower. The authors read this two ways: safety signals "fail to apply the brake", or fear generalises to anything that looks a bit similar. Once bitten by a dog, you're afraid even of the leashed little dog in your building: that's the idea.

The body plays along at the other end: the stress axis holds cortisol high for long stretches, heart-rate variability runs low, the body budget stays overdrawn. HPA stress axis This also explains why exposure therapy works: approaching the feared thing step by step under safe conditions gives the brain the chance to write that new "it was fine" memory.

Limits: animal experiments measure "anxiety-like behaviour" (a rat avoiding open spaces, say), which isn't the same as a human's experience of anxiety; brain-imaging differences are mostly group averages and can't diagnose any one person; and effect sizes are modest: the meta-analysis put excess fear to safety cues as a small effect, possibly present in only some patients.

🌀 Cross-disciplinary echoes

  • Stoicism · Seneca: in a letter to a friend Seneca wrote that we suffer more often in imagination than in reality. Two thousand years on, that line is almost a plain-language version of "anxiety is over-prediction of what might happen". The Stoic remedy was to separate what is in our control from what isn't and concern ourselves only with the former, which sits close to today's treatments aimed at intolerance of uncertainty.
  • Buddhism · the second arrow: the Sallatha Sutta ("The Arrow") says an ordinary person, struck by a first arrow (bodily pain), shoots themselves with a second (distress and resistance about the pain), and so suffers twice. Worry and avoidance are much like that second arrow: not the threat itself but our reaction to a possible threat, and it hurts all the same.
  • Philosophy · Kierkegaard: in The Concept of Anxiety Kierkegaard called anxiety "the dizziness of freedom": the vertigo of standing before endless possibilities without knowing which way to go. Fear is aimed at a specific object; anxiety faces "anything could happen", an echo across more than a century of the neuroscientific point that anxiety has no target.

// Ten takeaways

  1. Fear targets clear, imminent threats; anxiety targets vague, unpredictable or distant ones.
  2. In rats, Davis found that fast-rising, fast-fading fear relies mainly on the central amygdala, and sustained anxiety more on the BNST.
  3. Later research suggests the central amygdala and the BNST don't divide the work absolutely and often act together.
  4. Moderate anxiety is a normal system for preparing for possible trouble; it becomes a problem when too strong, too lasting, or disruptive.
  5. De Berker found in 2016 that people were most stressed when the chance of a shock was 50%, and least at 0% and 100%.
  6. Anxious people tend to overestimate the probability and cost of bad outcomes and find uncertainty especially hard to bear.
  7. Avoidance brings instant relief, and that negative reinforcement breeds more avoidance while blocking the discovery that things were fine.
  8. Worry feels like control: Borkovec held that it damps emotional processing; contrast avoidance holds that it keeps tension steadily moderate.
  9. Fear extinction doesn't erase the old memory; it writes a new safety memory, with the ventromedial prefrontal cortex taking part.
  10. Anxiety patients fear safety cues more and extinguish more slowly, and exposure therapy gives the brain a chance to learn safety.

// Think deeper

What practical difference does separating fear from anxiety make?
It reminds us that what anxiety needs to handle is often not a particular thing but uncertainty itself. That's why reassurance like "don't worry, nothing will happen" often fails: it doesn't remove the uncertainty, it just offers another prediction. What helps more is building tolerance for uncertainty, or turning a vague worry into a concrete question that can be tested.
If avoidance makes anxiety stickier, should you force yourself to face everything?
Not by charging in. Exposure therapy emphasises "gradual, controlled, safe": start at a level you can bear, step it up bit by bit, and stay until the anxiety subsides on its own, so the brain gets the chance to write "it was fine". Leaping straight into the scariest situation and fleeing halfway may deepen the fear instead.
Is worry completely useless?
Part of what feels like worry is really planning: spotting a possible problem, then doing something about it. The difference is whether it leads to action and whether it stops. The kind to watch is the loop that keeps returning to the start and winds you up further. A simple test: ask yourself "what's the next step I can take right now?" If you can't answer, you're probably spinning your wheels.
Is a more "cautious" AI always better?
Not necessarily. Worst-case-focused strategies are valuable in high-stakes settings, but in everyday decisions they miss a lot of good outcomes. Same for people: the value and cost of anxiety depend on how dangerous the environment really is. The right degree of pessimism should adjust to the environment, and anxiety's problem is precisely that it won't adjust back.
