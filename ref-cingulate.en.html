The Cingulate CortexCingulate Cortex

A curved band of cortex hugging the corpus callosum (the thick bundle of fibres linking the two hemispheres); the name comes from the Latin for "belt". The anterior cingulate, dorsal anterior cingulate, subgenual area 25 and posterior cingulate mentioned here and there across this site are all segments of this one belt, and they do very different jobs.

One belt, several regions: why the names are such a mess

Old textbooks followed Brodmann and simply cut it into an "anterior" and a "posterior" half. Later the anatomist Brent Vogt, combining evidence from cell architecture, fibre connections, receptor distributions and electrical stimulation, found that split too coarse and proposed a four-region model: anterior cingulate (subdivided into subgenual and pregenual), midcingulate (anterior and posterior), posterior cingulate, and retrosplenial cortex. Receptor mapping of post-mortem brains also supports four regions rather than two.

The trouble is that both naming systems are still in use. What many papers call the "dorsal anterior cingulate" (dACC) is, in the four-region model, mostly the "anterior midcingulate" (aMCC). So when you read that "the dACC activates during conflict, pain and effort", it's essentially the aMCC.

Subgenual anterior cingulate, sACC (area 25)
Below the "knee" (genu) at the front of the corpus callosum, tightly linked to the hypothalamus, amygdala and brainstem autonomic centres; regulates mood and bodily state. It is often persistently overactive in depression, settles after effective treatment, and is one target of deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression (main track, Topic 24).
Pregenual anterior cingulate, pACC
Appraising and regulating emotion: is this good or bad for me, should I tone it down. Closely tied to the amygdala and medial prefrontal cortex.
Anterior midcingulate, aMCC (≈ older "dACC")
Conflict monitoring, error detection, the unpleasantness of pain, and working out "is this worth the effort". With the anterior insula it forms the salience network (→ insula & interoception), and it connects directly to the motor system and to the systems that regulate the viscera. More on this below.
Posterior midcingulate, pMCC
Leans toward body orientation and skeletal motor control, closely connected to parietal and motor areas.
Posterior cingulate, PCC
The hub of the default mode network, handling autobiographical memory and self-perspective (→ default mode network).
Retrosplenial cortex, RSC
Below the back end of the corpus callosum, linked to the hippocampus; involved in spatial navigation and scene memory, aligning "where I am" with "the map" (main track, Topic 5).

aMCC: where "is it worth the effort?" gets worked out

Before doing something hard, the brain has to answer one question: is what I'll get worth the effort I'll spend? In 2013 Amitai Shenhav, Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen proposed the "expected value of control" theory: the aMCC weighs how much reward investing cognitive control would bring against what it would cost, then decides whether to push and how hard. It shows up in many experiments where people choose between "effortful but well-paid" and "easy but poorly paid".

The same year brought rare causal evidence. While Josef Parvizi and colleagues had electrodes implanted in two epilepsy patients to locate their seizures, they electrically stimulated one spot in the aMCC. Both patients reported a similar experience: a sense of a challenge ahead, together with a determination to push through it, along with a racing heart. Sham stimulation produced nothing, and neither did moving about 5 millimetres away. Only two patients, but a rare kind of evidence.

In a 2020 review, Alexandra Touroutoglou, Lisa Feldman Barrett and colleagues described the aMCC as a core hub of "the tenacious brain": one of the most widely connected regions of the brain, able to set "how much energy this will take" against "how much reward this will bring" and then direct bodily resources and attention toward the goal. It's the same logic as the body budget: effort spends budget, so something has to decide whether to spend it (→ body budget).

Where the popular claims overreach

"The aMCC is the willpower centre, and it grows when you do things you don't want to do" has spread widely in recent years. It goes too far:

"The harder you push yourself, the bigger it gets"
Direct evidence is thin. Cross-sectional studies find this region thicker or larger in "superagers" with exceptional memory and in successful dieters, but they can't tell whether persistence enlarged it or people with a larger one persist more. No randomised training study has shown that deliberately doing hard things makes the aMCC grow. The review's own authors present "thickness and connectivity may explain individual differences in persistence" as a hypothesis.
"The willpower centre"
Its functions are mixed: pain, conflict, errors, negative emotion and social exclusion all activate it. One unifying view is that it handles situations that call for mobilising resources to cope, and persistence is just one of them.
"It fights instinct and pleasure"
It isn't against pleasure; it's doing the accounts. When the reward is tempting enough, it will back you just as hard in chasing something enjoyable.
