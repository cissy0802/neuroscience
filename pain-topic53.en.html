The neuroscience of pain

Pain isn't a reading sent straight up from the wound; it's a conclusion the brain reaches

2026-11-02 · BigCat

A nail that never touched the skin hurt badly enough to need strong painkillers; a bullet wound left a soldier barely complaining.

We tend to picture pain as a wire: wherever you're hurt, a signal runs along the nerves to the brain, and the worse the injury, the worse the pain. Four hundred years ago Descartes drew it that way: a foot touches fire, it's like tugging a cord, and a bell rings in the head.

But that "bell-rope" can't explain a lot: a leg that's been amputated still hurts, a back still aches years after the injury healed, and when you're anxious everything feels uncomfortable. The central shift in modern pain science is this: pain is the brain's conclusion that "protection is needed", reached after weighing body signals, past experience, the present context and expectations. That doesn't mean pain is fake. Quite the opposite: it's real enough to be deeply rewritten by emotion, attention and learning.

// 01

Damage signals are not pain

Neuroscience keeps two things apart. Nerve endings in skin, muscle and organs detect stimuli that "might cause damage", such as heat, pressure or chemicals, and send signals upward: this is nociception. Pain is an experience. The International Association for the Study of Pain's 2020 definition: an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage. Note the word "emotional": by definition, pain is more than sensation.

The two often don't match. During the Second World War the American anaesthetist Henry Beecher recorded over two hundred severely wounded soldiers on the Italian front and found that about three-quarters had so little pain that they didn't ask for pain relief. For them, a wound meant leaving the battlefield alive. It works the other way too: in 1995 the British Medical Journal reported a builder who jumped onto a 15-centimetre nail that went through his boot; he was in such pain that he needed strong painkillers to be treated, and when the boot came off the nail turned out to have passed between his toes, without breaking the skin.

The most extreme case is phantom limb pain: most amputees feel pain in a leg that is no longer there, sometimes severe pain. No tissue, no nerve endings, yet the pain is real, because the place that produces pain was always the brain.

There's no single "pain centre" in the brain; several regions work together. Somatosensory cortex roughly judges where the pain is and how strong; the insula reads it alongside the body's overall state; the anterior midcingulate handles how unpleasant it feels and whether to mobilise to deal with it. // 02

The brain has a volume knob

Bang your knee and you rub it without thinking, and rubbing really does help a little. In 1965 Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall proposed gate control theory to explain this: at the entry point in the spinal cord where damage signals head upward, there's a "gate"; non-painful signals like touch and pressure partly close it. More importantly, the brain can also control that gate from above.

This top-down pathway runs through the brainstem, centred on the midbrain's periaqueductal grey. Brainstem & arousal systems It can dampen pain, and it can amplify it. Placebo pain relief is the "dampen" side: a 1978 study found that blocking the body's opioid receptors with naloxone largely weakened the placebo effect, showing that "believing it will help" mobilises the body's own opioid-like substances; a 2009 imaging study saw even more directly that placebo can reduce responses to painful stimuli at the level of the spinal cord, meaning the signal was turned down before it reached the brain.

Conversely, nocebo (expecting more pain and then actually feeling more), anxiety and over-attention turn the knob up. The anxiety of Topic 52 has one more consequence here: the same bodily signal, in a brain in threat mode, is felt as stronger pain.

// 03

From acute to chronic: pain can be "learned"

Acute pain is a useful alarm: stop using that sprained ankle. The trouble is that the alarm system itself can be changed by experience. Repeated or persistent damage signals make pain neurons in the spinal cord and brain more and more sensitive, which is called central sensitisation: touches that didn't hurt start to hurt, mild pain becomes severe, and the painful area spreads. By then the tissue may long since have healed, while the alarm keeps running at high sensitivity.

The brain's side of this "learning" is visible too. In 2012 Marwan Baliki and colleagues followed people whose back pain was only weeks old for a year. Who would go on to chronic pain was visible from the start: the stronger the connection between the nucleus accumbens and the medial prefrontal cortex, the more likely the person was still in pain a year later, and this measure predicted better than initial pain intensity. The nucleus accumbens is part of the reward and learning circuitry. Reward circuit This hints that chronic pain may to a large extent be the brain "learning" to treat signals from that body part as a threat. It's an observational study: connectivity is associated with chronicity, but that doesn't prove it causes it.

Behaviour learns too. The classic model in back-pain research is the fear-avoidance model (Vlaeyen and Linton 2000): you think of the pain as very serious ("my spine is damaged, bending will make it worse"), so you fear movement and move less and less; muscles and fitness decline, so moving hurts more easily, and the pain confirms the fear. It's the same structure as the avoidance trap in Topic 52.

AI parallel

Engineering has a class of systems called anomaly detection: monitoring servers, bank transactions or factory equipment and raising an alarm as soon as the data drifts from "normal". The core problem is where to set the threshold: set it high and you miss real problems; set it low and you get constant false alarms. If the system also tunes itself from past alarms, say "lots of trouble lately, let's be more sensitive", it can fall into a loop where more alarms bring more sensitivity and more sensitivity brings more alarms, until it alarms even on normal data. Chronic pain looks a lot like this threshold drift: the alarm isn't broken, just tuned too sensitive. The difference is that a machine's parameter can be changed directly, while a person's "threshold" has to be brought back slowly through new experience that shows, again and again, that "moving like this is fine".

// 04

Treatments aimed at the brain, and a few boundaries

If chronic pain is largely an alarm system that has learned the wrong thing, can it be taught back? In 2022 Yoni Ashar and colleagues published a randomised controlled trial in JAMA Psychiatry with 151 people with chronic back pain. One group received about four weeks of pain reprocessing therapy: helping patients understand their pain mainly as a brain "false alarm" rather than ongoing damage, and safely re-doing the movements they feared.

At the end of treatment, 66% of this group were "pain-free or nearly pain-free", compared with 20% in the placebo group and about 10% with usual care; the effects largely held a year later. It's a single study that needs replicating, but it shows that for much chronic back pain, the brain is a very powerful place to intervene. Exercise and pain-focused cognitive behavioural therapy also have good evidence, and combining them often works best.

A few boundaries need stating:

"Pain is constructed by the brain" doesn't mean "pain is fake" or "it's all in your head". All pain is constructed by the brain, including the pain of a broken bone. The difference is only that in some chronic pain, the main source of the alarm has shifted from the tissue to the alarm system itself.

Rule out what needs medical care first. Back pain with leg numbness or weakness, waking at night from pain, fever, unexplained weight loss, or bladder or bowel changes needs prompt medical attention.

About fascia. The fascia of the lower back does have rich pain-sensing nerve endings and can itself be a source of pain; fascia also contains cells that can slowly contract, which led to the hypothesis that "stress tightens fascia" (Schleip 2005). People with chronic low back pain have been found to have thicker lower-back fascia with less sliding between layers (Langevin 2011), but that's a correlation, quite possibly a result of moving less. There's no direct evidence yet that "anxiety causes back pain by tightening fascia"; the firmer routes from anxiety to back pain are the ones in this issue: muscle tension, top-down amplification and fear-avoidance.

🌀 Cross-disciplinary echoes

  • Philosophy · Descartes's bell-rope: in his Treatise of Man Descartes drew a figure kneeling by a fire: the flame tugs a fine thread in the foot, the thread runs all the way to the brain and opens a little pore, like pulling a rope to ring a bell. This "direct line" picture dominated ideas about pain for three centuries, until gate control theory and phantom limb research overturned it: there's a gate partway along the line, and the brain at the end reaches back to rewrite the signal.
  • Philosophy · Wittgenstein's beetle in a box: Wittgenstein used pain as the model of a "private experience": everyone has a box only they can look into, holding something called a "beetle", which no one else can ever see. Pain science is in exactly this position: no instrument can directly read how much pain someone else is in, and the most reliable measure is still the patient's own report, which is why "pain is real" has to begin with believing the patient.
  • Anthropology · Zborowski: in 1952 the anthropologist Mark Zborowski studied patients of different ethnic backgrounds in a New York hospital and found large differences in how people expressed pain and understood what it meant: some voiced it loudly and expected comfort, others endured in silence. Later work criticised his categories considerably, but his core point remains: the meaning and expression of pain are learned within a culture.

// Ten takeaways

  1. Nociception is nerve endings reporting possible damage; pain is an experience that includes emotion, and the two often don't match.
  2. Beecher found about three-quarters of severely wounded WWII soldiers didn't ask for pain relief; what the wound meant changed the pain.
  3. Phantom limb pain shows the brain can produce real, severe pain without tissue or nerve endings.
  4. Melzack and Wall's gate control theory holds that a spinal gate filters damage signals and the brain controls it from above.
  5. The descending pathway through the brainstem's periaqueductal grey can both dampen and amplify pain.
  6. Placebo pain relief mobilises the body's own opioid-like substances and can even reduce pain responses in the spinal cord.
  7. Central sensitisation makes pain pathways ever more sensitive, so ordinary signals can hurt after tissues heal.
  8. Baliki found in 2012 that nucleus accumbens–medial prefrontal connectivity predicts whether subacute back pain becomes chronic.
  9. In Ashar's 2022 trial, 66% of chronic back pain patients given pain reprocessing therapy were nearly pain-free after treatment.
  10. Fascia can be a pain source, but "anxiety causes back pain by tightening fascia" is still a hypothesis without direct evidence.

// Think deeper

If pain is constructed by the brain, why am I told to "get it checked"?
Because when the brain constructs pain, tissue signals are still an important input. Acute pain, and pain with warning signs, should first be checked for tissue causes; only once damage needing treatment has been ruled out and the pain persists does "the alarm system has been mistuned" become the main explanation. The two views aren't opposed; it's a question of order.
Does knowing that "chronic pain is mostly a brain false alarm" make it go away?
Knowing helps a little, but usually isn't enough. The key to pain reprocessing therapy isn't just explanation; it's safely re-doing feared movements so the brain gets new experience that "moving like this is fine". That's the same logic as exposure therapy in Topic 52: predictions have to be updated by new evidence.
Should I still take painkillers?
For acute pain, use them when needed; controlling pain helps you get moving again. For chronic pain, many guidelines put more weight on non-drug approaches like exercise and psychological therapy, and are very cautious about long-term opioid use, which can bring dependence and can even make people more sensitive to pain. Medication decisions belong with your doctor.
Anxiety and chronic pain: which to treat first?
They often feed each other, so it's best to tackle them together. The good news is that many approaches help both: regular exercise, better sleep, slow breathing, cognitive behavioural therapy, and breaking the avoidance habit. Seeing them as two expressions of the same threat system often works better than treating them separately.
