Amygdala

Called the "fear center" for decades, it's really the brain's relevance alarm: it doesn't specialize in fear so much as flag "this matters to me, pay attention" — whether that's danger, novelty, ambiguity, or a pleasant surprise.

First, kill a myth: it's not the "fear center"

Textbooks take a shortcut and dump fear wholesale onto the amygdala. But look at the data: the amygdala lights up when you're surprised, curious, excited, seeing a face with an ambiguous expression, even when something good happens; and people with amygdala damage don't simply stop knowing fear. The more accurate description — it's a salience / relevance detector: it rapidly estimates "how much does this have to do with me (especially with the safety of this body), and how uncertain is it," then decides whether to sound the alarm and mobilize the whole system. Fear is just its most frequently lit use, not the whole of it.

Two roads: the fast, crude "low road" and the slow, fine "high road"

Neuroscientist Joseph LeDoux sketched a classic dual pathway. There's no time to inspect danger carefully, so the brain would rather be scared first and check later:

The low road: the thalamus sends a crude signal by a shortcut straight to the amygdala, sounding the alarm in milliseconds — before you've even made out "is that a snake," the body has already flinched. The high road: the same signal detours through the cortex for a slow, careful identification, then sends the verdict back to the amygdala to double-check — "oh, it's a garden hose," alarm called off. The fast-and-crude road makes sure you don't miss a threat; the slow-and-fine road makes sure you're not always jumping at shadows. (This classic dual pathway has since been contested and is regarded as oversimplified, but as an intuition for "why the fear response can beat consciousness to the punch," it still serves well.)

What is it actually computing

Fear conditioning (its firmest day job)
Pair a neutral stimulus (a bell) repeatedly with a shock, and afterward the bell alone makes the heart race — the amygdala is the central hub of this kind of associative learning, and its best-evidenced function.
Uncertainty / ambiguity
The more unclear or unsure the situation (a face with a murky expression, an ambiguous predicament), the more active the amygdala — what it tracks is "does this need more of my attention," not any one specific emotion.
Not just bad things
Pleasant surprise, intense craving, and socially important cues (eye contact) light it up too. So the fitter portrait is a "significance" detector — positive and negative alike.
Wired to the body (meets interoception)
It projects down to the hypothalamus and brainstem, so sounding the alarm automatically adjusts heart rate, blood pressure, and freezing — translating "this matters" into bodily mobilization. This is one source of the body signals in emotion construction.

Seen back inside emotion construction

Treating it as the "fear center" is precisely the illusion left behind by the classical view of emotion. It fits far more smoothly inside the construction of emotion framework: what the amygdala supplies is not the finished product "fear" but an urgent "attention — this is important and uncertain" signal — it raises the arousal of core affect and hits the mobilize button on the body budget. Whether that heightened bodily state ends up constructed as fear, anger, or excitement depends on the concepts and context at hand, and that's the product of whole-brain teamwork, not the amygdala's call alone.
